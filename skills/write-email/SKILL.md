---
name: write-email
description: Draft a clear professional email or message that gets a reply. Use when the user asks to write, reply to, rewrite or follow up on an email, a work message or a note to a client, boss, colleague or stranger.
---

Goal: a message the reader understands in ten seconds and knows exactly what to do with.

**Steps**
1. Find out four things, from what the user gave you: who it goes to and how well they know them, what the user wants to happen after it is read, anything the reader already knows or said, and any fixed facts (dates, amounts, names). Ask for what is missing in one short message. If the user pasted an email to reply to, answer what it actually asks.
2. Put the point first. The first sentence says why you are writing or what you need. Background comes after, only as much as the reader needs.
3. Make one clear ask, with a date or time if there is one ("by Thursday"). If there are several asks, number them.
4. Match the tone to the relationship: warm and brief for a colleague, courteous and complete for a client or someone senior, plain and polite for a stranger.
5. Write a subject line that says what the email is about and, if needed, the action ("Approval needed by Friday: venue contract").

**Output**
- Subject line, then the email, ready to copy.
- If the situation is delicate (a refusal, a complaint, bad news, money), add one softer and one more direct version, labelled.

**Rules**
- Keep it short: most emails fit in under 120 words. Cut greetings that say nothing and sentences that only repeat.
- Never invent facts, promises, discounts or deadlines. If a detail is missing, leave a clear [bracket] for the user to fill.
- Do not send anything. If the user has an email app connected and asks you to send it, show the final text and the recipients and wait for a yes first.
- No threats, guilt or sarcasm, even when the user is annoyed. Say it firmly and politely.
